What one simulation does

1

Place vessels in a virtual voxel: how many, how big, which way they point

2

Compute the field each vessel creates. Deoxygenated blood is slightly magnetic

3

Let water diffuse through the field and add up the signal over time

Chaussé, Berman & Chen, Imaging Neuroscience 2025, Fig. 4a–c (spin echo, TE 70 ms)

3

Here's what a single simulation looks like, from the toolbox paper. On the left is a virtual voxel: a slice through randomly oriented vessels, like a capillary bed. In the middle is the magnetic field around them. Deoxygenated blood is slightly magnetic, so each vessel bends the field nearby. That's the red and blue pattern. Then the simulation lets water molecules wander through that field. Each one picks up a slightly different phase, they fall out of sync, and the signal drops. On the right you can see the spin-echo signal: it decays, the refocusing pulse brings part of it back, and then it decays again. They also split it into the tissue part and the blood part. For a dictionary, you just repeat this for every combination of oxygenation, blood volume and radius.
