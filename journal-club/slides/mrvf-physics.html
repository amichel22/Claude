Why one curve can report SO₂, CBV and R

SO₂

Less oxygen means more paramagnetic deoxyhemoglobin and a stronger field around each vessel.

CBV

More vessels put more of the voxel inside those fields.

R

Near small vessels, water diffuses through the field, so the 180° only partly undoes the dephasing. Near large vessels the field a spin sees barely changes, so the spin echo recovers.

This animation is my attempt to show why. I simulated it for this talk, using the field around a single vessel, with T2 decay turned off. Deoxyhemoglobin is paramagnetic, so a vessel carrying deoxygenated blood makes a small field around itself. The colors in the background show that field. Each dot is a water spin, colored by its phase. On the left is a 3-micron vessel, and on the right a 30-micron one, drawn at a tenth the scale. Watch the trace under each one. Both signals drop at first as the spins dephase. At 50 milliseconds, the 180-degree pulse flips the phases. Around the large vessel, the field barely changes over the distance a spin diffuses, so each spin sees nearly the same field before and after the 180, and the phases line up again. By the spin echo, the signal is back to about 0.92. Around the small vessel, water diffuses through the field, so the 180 only partly undoes the dephasing. The signal ends near 0.75. So SO2 sets the field strength, CBV sets how much of the voxel feels it, and vessel size sets how much the spin echo recovers.
